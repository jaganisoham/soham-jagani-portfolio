Fiberglass Dust Vacuum System

A duct-and-frame vacuum pickup designed to capture fiberglass dust at the source on a roofing production line — protecting nearby bearings and rollers from abrasive buildup and reducing airborne irritant exposure for operators.

§01

Overview

On the fiberglass sheet line used to make roofing shingles, fine glass fiber dust settles continuously on nearby equipment. Over time it works into bearings and rollers, accelerating wear and shortening service life — and it's also a skin and respiratory irritant for anyone working near the line.

The goal was a vacuum pickup that could be mounted directly at the dust source: pull the fiberglass dust away before it settles, route it through ducting to a drain/collection point, and do it with a structure that could survive constant vibration and abrasive dust exposure. The system was designed, fabricated, and installed — and it worked, measurably cutting dust buildup on the equipment it was mounted over.

§02

Design & Engineering

The full assembly was modeled in SolidWorks: a 4" ABS Schedule 40 pipe pair (a slotted "bottom pipe" for intake and a solid "top pipe") supported on a welded structure arm-and-leg frame, braced with Unistrut-style strut channel, and routed through pipe elbows into an above-ground PVC drain fitting for disposal.

Every part of the frame and ducting is called out in a full SolidWorks bill of materials, mixing off-the-shelf hardware with the two custom-machined pipes:

§03

Engineering Considerations

Beyond laying out geometry, a handful of first-principles checks shaped the design decisions below.

AIRFLOW

Conveying Velocity (FPM)

For a vacuum duct to actually carry fiberglass dust instead of letting it settle and clog the line, the air moving through the pipe has to stay above a minimum conveying velocity for a light, fibrous material. Sizing the required airflow starts from the duct's actual cross-section — pulled straight from the bottom pipe's print (4.000" ID):

A = (π/4)·D² = (π/4)·(4/12 ft)² ≈ 0.0873 ft²
Q = A · v

Targeting a conveying velocity in the ~3,500–4,500 FPM range typical for fine, light dust puts the required flow at roughly 300–390 CFM through that 4" duct — the number the drain-side hardware and elbow routing needed to be sized to move without the pipe becoming a settling trap.

MATERIAL PHYSICS

Static Buildup in the Pipe

Dry fiberglass dust moving at speed through a non-conductive ABS duct generates a triboelectric charge, the same effect that charges up plastic ductwork in any dry-material dust collection system. Left unmanaged, that charge attracts dust back onto the pipe walls (undoing the whole point of the vacuum) and can build to an uncomfortable static shock at any exposed metal fitting. The metal routing clamps and hardware at each duct-to-frame transition were treated as bonding points to give any accumulated charge a path off the ductwork.

STRUCTURAL

Hoop Stress & the Slot/Tab Pattern

The bottom pipe needed a continuous row of intake slots cut into its wall — but a pipe under vacuum is a thin-wall pressure vessel, and its resistance to collapsing depends on keeping a continuous hoop of material around its circumference:

σ_hoop = ΔP · r / t

Cutting one continuous slot down the pipe's length would interrupt that hoop path entirely at the cut, leaving the section prone to ovaling or collapsing under the vacuum's negative pressure. The print instead breaks the intake into a series of short 2.125" slots separated by 0.375" solid tabs every 12" — short bridges of material that keep the pipe's hoop stiffness intact along its full length while still opening enough area for airflow to enter.

MATERIAL SELECTION

Durability

ABS Schedule 40 pipe was chosen over metal ducting as the balance point between abrasion resistance and cost: fiberglass dust is abrasive enough to wear through thin sheet metal over time, but a thick-wall ABS pipe holds up to that wear while staying easy to machine (slots, drilled holes) with standard shop tools, and is far cheaper and lighter than a comparable stainless assembly.

BUDGET

Cost

Nearly every part in the BOM outside the two custom pipes is an off-the-shelf McMaster-Carr or Grainger component — standard Unistrut channel, caps, and routing clamps; standard elbows and drain fittings; standard hex hardware. Building the frame from stock components instead of custom-fabricated brackets kept both the material cost and the lead time down, appropriate for a plant-maintenance-scale project.

§04

Results

The system was fabricated and installed on the line. It successfully pulled fiberglass dust away from the equipment it was mounted over, reducing the buildup that had been accelerating bearing and roller wear, and cutting down on airborne dust near the work area.
