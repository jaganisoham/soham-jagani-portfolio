MAE 435 — PRINCIPLES OF AUTOMATIC CONTROL

PID Control of a Thruster-Actuated Inverted Pendulum

Modeled, linearized, and controlled a pneumatic thruster-driven arrow rotating on a fixed pivot — from nonlinear equations of motion through Root-Locus controller design to validation on physical hardware.

TERM
Spring 2026
SOFTWARE
MATLAB, Simulink
DOMAIN

TYPE

§01

Overview

The plant is a thruster-actuated arrow ("Wheel of Despair") that rotates freely about a fixed pivot. Two pneumatic thrusters, driven by current through an amplifier, push the arrow toward a target angle. The objective was to design a controller that could rotate the arrow to a set of labeled positions — 100°, 180°, and 260° (−100°) — while limiting overshoot and oscillation, then validate that controller on the physical rig.

The system is nonlinear on two fronts: gravitational torque depends on sin(θ), and the thruster force-to-current relationship is itself nonlinear. The full project moved through modeling, linearization, Root-Locus controller design, Simulink simulation, and real hardware testing.

§02

System Modeling

Starting from a free-body diagram of the arrow, the equation of motion accounts for gravity, a damping torque, and the thruster force acting at a moment arm:

J·θ̈ + B·θ̇ = F_t·L2 − L1·m·g·sin(θ)

The two unknowns — thrust as a function of current, F(i), and the damping coefficient B — were identified from experimental data. Each thruster's step response was fit to a piecewise quadratic with a deadband, since the two thrusters had noticeably different response curves:

F(i) = 15.2i² − 9.09i + 1.36 (i > 0.33)
F(i) = 0 (−0.33 ≤ i ≤ 0.33)
F(i) = −8.91i² − 3.81i − 0.351 (i < −0.33)

Damping was first estimated from the free response using logarithmic decrement (B = 0.0212 Ns/m), but this damped out faster than the real system. Comparing simulated free response against real free-response data showed a better match at B = 0.015 Ns/m — the discrepancy was attributed to static friction and hose spring force that the model didn't capture.

§03

Controller Design

The plant was linearized about θ = 0° in MATLAB, producing the transfer function:

G(s) = 25.64 / (s² + 0.5632s + 3.913)

The linearized plant was stable but couldn't reduce steady-state error to zero and showed significant overshoot. Using MATLAB's Control System Designer, an integrator was added to eliminate steady-state error, a zero at −0.3 for stability, and a pole/zero pair (−25, −3) to shape the root locus — targeting a rise time under 5s, settling time under 20s, and 5% overshoot. The final controller:

C(s) = 11.228 · (s + 0.3)(s + 3) / [s(s + 25)]
§04

Results

The controller was simulated in Simulink at 100°, 180°, and −100° before touching hardware. It hit 0 steady-state error at ±100°, but was only marginally stable at 180° with sustained oscillation — acceptable, since ±100° stability was the primary goal.

On the physical rig, the results were better than simulation predicted: the arrow stabilized with zero steady-state error at all three setpoints, including 180°, which had only been marginally stable in simulation.

Real-world settling time (4.5s at −100°) ran slightly longer than simulation (3.5s), with more visible oscillation — again consistent with unmodeled static friction and hose spring effects. The simulation captured the plant's general dynamics well, just not every physical detail.

§05

Hardware

The rig — nicknamed the "Wheel of Despair" — mounts a 3D-printed arrow on a low-friction pivot, with two pneumatic thrusters supplied by braided air hoses. Labeled zones around the wheel mark the target angles.

§06

Conclusion

The controller successfully stabilized the arrow at every target region, including the 180° case that simulation had flagged as only marginally stable. The main challenge was deciding what angle to linearize about; once resolved, the resulting transfer function represented the plant well enough to carry a working PID design from MATLAB straight through to hardware.

The gap between simulated and real settling behavior — attributed to static friction and hose spring force — was a reminder that a controller that works in theory still has to be tested: simplifying assumptions and real hardware both shape final performance.
