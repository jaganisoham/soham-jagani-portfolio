Kirigami Gripper for Sweet Potato Stem Planting

A soft, kirigami-cut gripper designed to grip and lift stem-like structures for automated planting — extending NC State's kirigami robotics research from delicate, precision grasping into agricultural automation.

Soham Jagani, Caizhi Zhou — Advisor: Dr. Jie Yin

NC State REU

§01

Objectives

  • Design a kirigami gripper that can grip and lift stem-like structures.
  • Improve pinching force while maintaining flexibility.
  • Develop a functional prototype capable of soil planting.
§02

Introduction

Kirigami is the art of cutting and folding paper into three-dimensional designs — origami's sibling, with the addition of cutting. NC State's Yin Lab is a leading center for kirigami-based robotics, known for soft, flexible mechanical systems that flex and reconfigure in ways origami-only designs can't.

Grippers developed in the lab have been delicate enough to lift a water droplet or a single strand of hair. This project extends that line of research from precision grasping toward a much rougher target: agricultural automation, where a gripper has to be gentle enough not to crush a stem but strong enough to plant it in soil.

LAB CONTEXT

This project builds on kirigami-gripper work published by the Yin Lab, which showed that cutting parallel slits into a 2D sheet and controlling the shape of its outer boundary lets the sheet pop into a curved 3D gripping structure when stretched — precise enough in that work to lift an egg yolk without breaking it. Dr. Jie Yin, an Associate Professor in NC State's Department of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering, received a 2025 Presidential Early Career Award for Scientists and Engineers (PECASE) for this line of kirigami-metamaterials research. More at jieyin.wordpress.ncsu.edu.

§03

Methods

FABRICATION

Each gripper starts as a 2D sketch in SolidWorks: a parallel-slit cut pattern roughly 90mm long and 70mm wide, with overall size, cut length, pattern, and uncut border width all treated as variables to test grip strength. The pattern is laser cut from a polycarbonate sheet a few millimeters thick — a material that can be swapped out to test how thickness and stiffness affect bending and force transfer.

TESTING

Grip performance was tested by manually pulling on the tabs and closing the gripper around stem-like objects — pencils were used as a stand-in for their similar diameter, testing both pinch force and the gripper's ability to rotate the object. Anti-slip tape was added at the contact points to increase friction and pinch force.

§04

Results

Updated kirigami cut patterns successfully gripped and lifted cylindrical objects like a pencil, maintaining stable contact across the surface and showing adaptability to stem-like shapes. Improved pinch force came from reinforced cut patterns and optimized geometry — repeated trials showed consistent lifting without slippage under light loads.

GRIP TYPE
Cylindrical, stem-like
SLIPPAGE
None across repeated trials
LOAD REGIME
Light loads, stable lift
§05

Discussion

  • Pinching force has improved through design iterations, but is still below the threshold needed for soil insertion.
  • Cut-pattern modifications — changes to spacing and alignment — helped concentrate force near the gripping tips.
  • Material thickness plays a crucial role: thicker materials offer more force but reduce flexibility.
  • Textured surfaces improved friction and grip consistency across various stem sizes.
  • Despite improved rotation and gripping, planting action remains inconsistent, especially when the gripper encounters soil resistance.
§06

Future Steps

  • Test alternative materials with higher stiffness and friction to increase pinching force and improve grip in soil.
  • Simulate gripping dynamics using finite element analysis to better understand stress distribution and force transfer.
  • Continue prototyping with refined kirigami patterns to balance flexibility and strength.
  • Conduct field tests to evaluate the gripper's effectiveness in real agricultural settings.
§07

Conclusions

  • The kirigami gripper's pinch force is significantly higher than earlier iterations.
  • The gripper can pick up stem-like objects from the ground without added clearance.
  • The gripper now consistently grips and rotates stems.
  • Minimizing the distance the gripper must move to pinch the object has greatly increased pinch force, translating more of the applied force into pinching rather than bending.
